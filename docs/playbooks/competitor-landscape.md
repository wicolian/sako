# Competitor landscape: productized design subscriptions

A map of the "unlimited design for a flat monthly fee" category as we saw it when we planned and sold our service (late 2025). It merges two research documents.

## How to use this

- Use it to understand how this category works before you enter it, or as a template for mapping your own category.
- Prices and plans change often. Treat every price below as a snapshot from our research. Check each company's site before you rely on it.
- Do the same exercise for your market: list every way a buyer can solve the problem today, not only direct rivals.

## 1. The category in one minute

Productized design subscriptions replace agencies, freelancers and in-house hires with a flat monthly fee. You send requests. A team delivers them. The model started around 2017, when a solo founder-run studio made it popular.

The promise is predictable cost, fast turnaround and scalable output without hiring.

The catch: "unlimited" is almost never unlimited. Most services give you one to three active slots. You can submit many requests, but they are done in order. Request number five waits behind requests one to four. Advertised turnaround is for one request, not for your queue.

What "unlimited" really means, by model:

| Model | What you really buy |
|---|---|
| Single active request | One designer, a few hours a day, one job at a time |
| Hours per day | A time budget, not a set of outputs. A hard task eats the day. |
| Credits | Different tasks cost different credits. Hard to predict what you get. |
| Active slots with an SLA | A known number of jobs in progress, each with a delivery clock |

We chose the last model.

## 3. Where we sat on the map

| | Cheap and many | Mid-market | Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Players | ManyPixels, Penji, Kimp | Design Pickle, ReelUnlimited, us | DesignJoy, Superside |

Our plans started well above the budget services and well below the premium ones. We bet that a higher price would signal quality and that buyers would pay for design and motion together.

## 4. What we claimed (our unique angles)

1. **Design and motion together from the first plan.** Competitors treat motion as an add-on or a separate service.
2. **An SLA, not an "average".** A 48-hour delivery promise on every active task, with service credits if missed.
3. **Structured intake plus chat updates.** One form captures the brief. Updates arrive in chat. Most rivals use a dashboard only, or loose chat.
4. **Clear active slots.** One to three, depending on plan. Buyers know what "in progress" means.
5. **Pause and bank days.** More flexible than a one-year contract or a plain "pause anytime".
6. **A middle plan.** Many rivals jump from one or two tasks straight to enterprise. We had a plan in between.
7. **AI credits included.** Useful for first drafts. Treat this as a nice extra, not the main reason to buy.

## 5. Where we were weaker (be honest about this)

- No brand recognition. The leaders had years of presence.
- No case studies and little social proof at the start.
- A higher entry price than the budget services, so price-sensitive buyers went elsewhere.
- Fewer guarantee options than the best rivals (some offered 30 days, trials or promotions).
- No website development, white-label resale or print.
- A small team. Very high-volume clients were a risk.
- No enterprise features such as 24/7 global coverage.

Write this list for your own business. Say it to yourself before you pitch.

## 6. Who we aimed at (segments)

| Segment | Size | Typical need | Plan we suggested |
|---|---|---|---|
| B2B SaaS, seed to Series B | 10 to 150 people | 5 to 15 design tasks and 3 to 5 motion assets a month | Middle plan |
| AI tools | 5 to 50 people | Weekly landing pages, constant ad tests | Core, then middle plan |
| Developer tools | 10 to 100 people | Docs, blog graphics, launch assets | Core |
| Fintech (non-regulated content) | 20 to 200 people | Weekly ads, motion to explain complex ideas | Middle plan |
| Consumer apps | 5 to 100 people | Daily social, weekly ads, store assets | Middle plan |
| Fast-moving direct-to-consumer brands | 5 to 50 people | Weekly emails, daily social, landing pages | Core or middle plan |
| Teams with one overwhelmed designer | 10 to 100 people | 10 to 20 routine tasks a month | Middle plan |

In the end the sales playbook narrowed this to B2B SaaS growth teams. A smaller list is easier to sell to. See `sales-playbook.md`.

## 7. Answers to common competitive objections

- **"We have a freelancer."** Freelancers are great for projects. For steady output, you manage their availability, revisions and invoices. They go on holiday. They rarely do motion.
- **"Another service is cheaper."** Check what you compare. Hours are not outputs. Extra fees for motion and platform add up. Compare finished tasks a month.
- **"We already have a designer."** You do not replace them. You take routine work (social, ads, decks) off their plate so they can do brand and product work.
- **"Slack is enough."** Chat is for talking. A queue with timers and revision history is for managing work.
- **"Do you have case studies?"** Say "not yet", then offer a fast trial on one real asset.
- **"Can you match our brand?"** Collect a brand kit at onboarding. Use it on every task.

## 8. Metrics we planned to track (targets, not results)

- Average tasks per client per month: 8 to 12 on the middle plan.
- SLA hit rate: 95% or more of tasks inside 48 hours.
- Revision cycles per task: fewer than 2 on average.
- Monthly churn: under 5%. A pause is fine because it keeps lifetime value.
- Upgrade rate from Core to the middle plan to Enterprise.

## 9. Template: map your own category

1. List 10 to 15 alternatives. Include DIY, freelancers and "do nothing".
2. For each: model, price, strengths, weaknesses, and what customers complain about (read public reviews).
3. Plot them on one price and quality map.
4. Write the 5 to 7 things you can claim that are true and checkable.
5. Write the list of things you are weaker at. Plan your pitch around them.
6. Turn each weakness into an objection and write your honest answer.
7. Repeat every quarter. Prices change.

## Lessons

- The strongest weakness of every rival was a vague promise ("average", "typical", "unlimited"). A clear promise is a real edge.
- A clear edge still did not remove the need for proof. Without case studies, sales relied on founder attention and a money-back guarantee.
- Research does not replace distribution. See the README.
