# Pitch and story: a deck template you can copy

How we told the story of the business to investors and partners. We wrote two versions: one pitched a creative operations business with software margins, and one pitched an operating system for productized services. This playbook gives you the slide structure, the lines that worked, and the traps.

## How to use this

- Copy the slide list in section 3. Fill in the brackets with your own facts.
- Use section 2 to choose a story. One story per deck.
- Read section 5 before you put any number on a slide.

## 1. Read this first: plans versus reality

Our decks contained ambitious projections: many delivery pods, large yearly revenue and very high margins. None of that happened.

What did happen: we started at the end of 2024, had no revenue until September 2025, then earned about $7,000 a month for about eight months, with 10% to 40% revenue growth. We closed around April 2026.

The lesson for your deck: investors fund evidence. Put real numbers first. Put forecasts in a clearly marked "plan" section, and say what the plan depends on.

## 2. Two stories we told

### Story A: "Creative operations with software margins"

- **Claim:** creative services can be sold like a subscription, with software-like predictability.
- **Problem:** agencies are slow, unpredictable, and expensive to scale. Clients wait for work they need in days. Agencies struggle with thin margins, burnout and fixed cost.
- **Solution:** subscription plans with an SLA, pooled capacity, and AI-assisted routing. Clients see a simple plan. Behind it, designers sit in a shared pool that scales with demand.
- **Model:** monthly plans. Pause anytime. No long contracts. We earn retention through quality.
- **Big idea line:** "the cloud of creative operations".

### Story B: "An operating system for productized services"

- **Claim:** thousands of creators will launch productized service subscriptions, but the tools to run them do not exist.
- **Problem:** providers run on a patchwork of docs, chat, billing, sheets and automations. That means unpredictable delivery, hidden scope creep, manual pricing, inaccurate estimates, missed SLAs and no view of throughput.
- **Shift:** AI sped up creative output. It did not fix service operations.
- **Solution:** describe a service and the system generates pricing, limits, SLA timers, intake forms, templates, checkout and capacity settings. Then one loop runs the work: sell, intake, deliver, bill, measure, improve.
- **Proof:** we ran our own design business on the system.
- **Big idea line:** "the same shift that commerce platforms brought to online stores, but for services".

Choose one. If you try to tell both, you end up with neither.

## 3. Slide template

Use 10 to 14 slides. One idea per slide. Large type. Few words.

1. **Title.** Company, one-line description, founder names.
2. **The promise.** The outcome the buyer gets, in a short sentence. Add three proof points (for example: delivery model, margin model, supply model).
3. **The problem.** Two columns: what clients suffer, what providers suffer. Add one number per column, with a source.
4. **The shift.** What changed in the world that makes this possible now.
5. **The solution.** What you built. Three or four parts, one line each.
6. **How it works.** Show the client view and the behind-the-scenes view side by side.
7. **What makes it different.** Three to five points that are true and checkable. For us: SLA on every task, capacity limits per client, chat-native intake, estimates from real delivery data.
8. **Pricing.** Your plan ladder in one table: plan, capacity, delivery promise, price, ideal client.
9. **Unit economics.** What one client or one delivery team costs and earns. Label every number: measured, target or assumed.
10. **Market.** Who buys. Start with a small, specific group you can name, then show how it grows.
11. **Traction.** Real numbers only. For us this would be: time to first revenue, monthly revenue, months at that level, growth rate, and delivery turnaround.
12. **Roadmap.** What you will do next, in quarters, tied to money and proof.
13. **Team.** Why you. What each founder owns. What you have built before.
14. **The ask.** What you want, and what the money buys.

### Pricing slide example (our shape)

| Plan | Active tasks | Turnaround | Price per month | Ideal client |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Core | 1 | 48 hours | $2,500 | Small teams with steady needs |
| Ops Base | 2 | 48 hours | $4,500 | Parallel workstreams |
| Enterprise | 3 or more | Custom | From $8,500 | Many teams, governance |

Add the three "no fear" lines: pause anytime, no long contracts, upgrade on demand.

### Traction slide template

> [Months] from first idea to first revenue.
> [Amount] a month for [number] months.
> [Growth] over that time.
> Average delivery: [time]. SLA hit rate: [percent].
> What we learned: [one sentence].

Use your real numbers, even when they are small. A true small number beats a large guess.

### Team slide template

For each founder: name, role, two or three things they built or ran, and why that matters for this business. In our case, one founder ran a team of designers and delivery. The other built product and ran growth, and built all the software with AI agents.

## 4. Lines we used (and why they help)

- "We run our own business on our own system." It shows you use what you sell.
- "Clients see a simple plan. Behind the scenes, there is a system." A clear split between promise and machinery.
- "AI changed output. It did not change operations." A fresh angle on a crowded topic.
- "Productivity, not replacement." About how AI is used in the team. It calms fears.

## 5. Traps (what we would do differently)

- **Numbers that disagree.** Our documents carried different revenue and price figures. An investor will find the gap. Keep one fact sheet and copy from it.
- **Projections without a base.** A forecast needs a measured starting point. We had a small one.
- **Too many ideas.** We pitched a services business and a software platform in the same breath. Pick one for each deck.
- **Market size slides with huge numbers.** They add little. A named, reachable first group is more convincing.
- **Hiring plans before sales evidence.** Investors read the plan as a cost, not a promise.
- **Hype words.** We loved big comparisons to cloud and commerce platforms. They get attention, but they do not replace traction.

## 6. Use of funds: template categories

List your categories and put a percentage on each. Ours were:

- Delivery team build and salaries
- Sales and marketing
- Product and AI development
- HR, finance and operations
- Equipment and infrastructure

Add: how many months of runway this buys, and the one milestone it gets you to.

## 7. A short pitch you can say out loud (fill in)

> [Buyers] lose [time or money] because [problem].
> We give them [outcome] with [model], delivered in [time], for [price].
> We run our own [business] on it. In [months] we went from zero to [revenue] a month.
> We are [two founders with what skills]. We want [ask] to reach [milestone].

## Adapt it

- If you are not raising money, use the same structure for a partner or customer deck. Replace "ask" with "next step".
- Keep a one-page version for people who will not open a deck.
- Update the deck every month. A stale traction slide is worse than none.
